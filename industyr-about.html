About Us

Who we are, what we do, why it matters

industyr of the ordinary emerged in 2019 from a series of informal gatherings in Chicago's South Side industrial corridor. What began as documentation of abandoned factories evolved into a sustained inquiry: what aesthetic languages emerge from spaces of labor? What happens when we treat the ordinary infrastructure of production as worthy of the same attention traditionally reserved for monuments?

We are artists, researchers, and workers committed to practices that refuse the separation between thinking and making, between concept and craft. Our methods are borrowed equally from ethnography, sculpture, performance, and process engineering.

Philosophy

The ordinary contains multitudes

The things we overlook—factory floors, shipping pallets, time cards, safety equipment—are laden with meaning. They encode histories of labor, gesture, and embodied knowledge. They reveal systems we've learned not to see.

Our practice begins with attention. Long, patient observation of sites and systems. We're interested in what emerges when you spend 40 hours watching a loading dock, when you trace the biography of a single bolt, when you ask workers to describe the sound of a machine they've operated for years.

This isn't nostalgia for industrial decline or romanticism of labor. It's an attempt to develop aesthetic vocabularies adequate to the complexity of how things actually get made, moved, maintained, and eventually discarded.

Approach

Collaboration as infrastructure

We work collectively, not as a fixed group but as a shifting network of practitioners who share methods and questions. Projects typically involve:

Site-based research

Extended residencies in active industrial sites—warehouses, fabrication shops, logistics hubs. We observe, document, and develop work in ongoing dialogue with the people who work these spaces.

Material investigation

We collect, catalog, and rework the byproducts of industrial processes. Offcuts, remnants, broken tools. Each object is a record of forces applied, a fragment of a larger system.

Performance and documentation

Translating industrial procedures into scores for performance. Creating archives that resist conventional hierarchies of what counts as knowledge or expertise.

Public engagement

Workshops, installations, and publications that make our research accessible beyond institutional art contexts. We believe this work belongs in union halls as much as galleries.

Core Members

Collective Structure

industyr of the ordinary operates as a horizontal collective. Decision-making is consensus-based, roles rotate, and labor—both artistic and administrative—is shared.

Current core members include artists, former factory workers, archivists, sound designers, and organizers. We meet monthly and collaborate on projects ranging from weeks to years.

Extended Network

Our work depends on partnerships with labor historians, union locals, independent fabricators, and community organizations across Chicago's industrial neighborhoods.

We maintain ongoing relationships with several South and West Side industrial sites that host our research and residency programs.

Location

Why Chicago

Chicago's identity is inseparable from its industrial history—stockyards, steel mills, rail yards, grain elevators. While much of that infrastructure has vanished or transformed, the city remains a crucial node in logistics networks, with active manufacturing, warehousing, and distribution still shaping neighborhoods and lives.

This makes it an ideal context for our work: a place where industrial process isn't historical artifact but living practice, where you can still find sites of making alongside sites of memory.

Our studio is located in Pilsen, with research sites throughout the South and West sides. We're committed to working within and for the communities where this labor happens, not as extractive researchers but as neighbors and collaborators.

Values

Operating principles

Anti-hierarchy

We resist the traditional art world's valorization of individual genius. All work is credited collectively unless otherwise specified.

Reciprocity

When we work with a site or community, we're accountable to those relationships. Documentation is shared, compensation is fair, and workers have final say on representation.

Accessibility

Art shouldn't require specialized knowledge to access. We prioritize clear language, free public events, and materials that circulate outside institutional walls.

Slowness

Good research takes time. We resist the pressure to constantly produce, instead working at the pace required by sustained attention.

Materiality

Physical materials matter. We work with objects, spaces, and bodies—not as metaphors but as things with weight, history, and resistance.

Contradiction

We hold tensions rather than resolving them. The industrial can be brutal and beautiful. The ordinary can be overlooked and essential. Both things are true.
